SBA loans · 7(a) & 504

The lowest rates in small business lending.

Government-backed 7(a) and 504 loans carry the longest terms and the lowest rates available to most small businesses, because the Small Business Administration guarantees part of the loan for the lender. They are also the slowest and the most documented route. Here's how each works, what it takes to qualify, and whether either fits.

The programs

Two programs, two jobs.

7(a) is the flexible, general-purpose program. 504 is built for buildings and long-life equipment. Most businesses fit one far better than the other.

7(a)

Up to $5M

The SBA's primary and most widely used program. The proceeds can go to more or less any legitimate business purpose.

Use it for

  • Working capital
  • Real estate acquisition, refinancing and improvements
  • Equipment financing: machinery, equipment, furniture, fixtures and supplies
  • Refinancing eligible existing business debt
  • Changes of ownership

504

Up to $5.5M SBA portion

Long-term, fixed-rate financing for major fixed assets, delivered through a Certified Development Company (CDC) alongside a bank.

Use it for

  • Buying land or buildings, construction and renovation
  • Equipment financing for long-term machinery and equipment with a useful life of 10+ years
  • Refinancing qualifying debt tied to those assets

Not for

  • Working capital or inventory
  • Repaying non-qualified debt, or speculation and investment in rental real estate

How 504 is built

The 50 / 40 / 10 split.

A 504 is not one loan. It is three pieces stacked, which is why the down payment is lower than a conventional commercial mortgage.

50%

Senior lender, such as a bank or credit union, provides half, secured by a first lien on the asset

40%

A Certified Development Company (CDC) funds this through an SBA-backed debenture at a long-term fixed rate, in second position

10%

You, as the capital injection. Ten percent is the standard case; see the exceptions below

  • 15% down if the business is under two years old, or the property is special-purpose.
  • 20% down if both apply.
  • Terms of 10, 20 or 25 years, matched to the useful life of what you are financing.
  • A special-purpose property is one with a limited resale market, such as a bowling alley or a winery, rather than a warehouse.

Standards

Who qualifies.

Every SBA loan starts from the same base test. Lenders then layer their own credit standards on top, which are usually stricter than the SBA's floor.

  • {{> icon-tick}}Operating as a for-profit business. Non-profits, passive holding companies and speculative ventures do not qualify
  • {{> icon-tick}}Based and trading in the US or its territories
  • {{> icon-tick}}Small by the SBA's size standards, which vary by industry and are set by revenue or employee count. Many companies that feel mid-sized still qualify
  • {{> icon-tick}}Not an ineligible business type. Lending, gambling, speculation and a defined list of other activities are excluded
  • {{> icon-tick}}Passes the "credit elsewhere" test: you must be unable to obtain the credit on reasonable terms from non-government sources. The SBA fills a gap; it is not designed to compete with your bank
  • {{> icon-tick}}Creditworthy and able to repay: demonstrated repayment ability, sound character, qualified management and a workable plan. This is where most applications are actually decided

Documents

What you will be asked for

SBA lending is document-heavy. Expect to produce most of the following, and expect the process to take weeks rather than days.

  • {{> icon-tick}}Business and personal tax returns, usually three years
  • {{> icon-tick}}Year-to-date profit and loss statement and balance sheet
  • {{> icon-tick}}Business bank statements
  • {{> icon-tick}}Personal financial statement for each owner of 20% or more
  • {{> icon-tick}}Debt schedule listing every existing obligation
  • {{> icon-tick}}Use of proceeds: precisely where the money goes
  • {{> icon-tick}}Business licenses, entity documents and leases
  • {{> icon-tick}}Personal guarantee from principal owners, and often collateral as well

Important

7(a) can't pay off cash advances.

Under the SBA's current lending rules, SOP 50 10 8, effective June 1, 2025, merchant cash advances and factoring agreements are not eligible for refinancing with SBA 7(a) proceeds. The restriction applies across every 7(a) variant: Standard 7(a), 7(a) Small, SBA Express, Export Express and International Trade. There is no workaround inside the program. The SBA's reasoning is that MCA structures do not sit well alongside the long-term, prudent financing the program exists to provide.

So what can you actually do?

  • Consolidate outside the SBA

    A conventional consolidation or a reverse consolidation can clear positions. Terms vary widely, so review them carefully with someone before signing.

  • Use home equity

    If you own property, a HELOC is often materially cheaper than an MCA and can be used to clear positions. Use your home equity.

  • Negotiate with funders

    Funders will often restructure a position rather than watch it default.

  • Use 7(a) where eligible

    A 7(a) for eligible purposes, such as equipment financing, property or eligible debt, frees up cash flow that can then service the advances down.
